02

Research

Better attention. Deeper reasoning. Massive label spaces, understood mechanistically.

Language models are increasingly asked to select a small set of relevant outputs from extremely large candidate spaces: diagnosis codes for a clinical note, topics for a legal document, products for a recommendation. Most labels have only a handful of labeled examples, and the task needs broad filtering combined with precise selection. Large models are surprisingly strong at this zero-shot; smaller models struggle. I study how the large models succeed, and how to pass that on to smaller ones.

Large output spaces

Choosing a document’s few labels from thousands to millions of candidates, when most labels have only a handful of training examples.

Papers: PLANT, MISTILL

Mechanistic interpretability of reasoning

Which components make a decision? I measure each attention head’s causal effect by removing it and transplanting it into another patient’s note. In clinical coding, a small, shared set of heads turns out to be necessary and sufficient on essentially every note.

Paper: MISTILL

Making smaller models better

Two routes. Attention pretraining: PLANT plants label-specific attention before fine-tuning begins. Mechanistic distillation: MISTILL teaches a student where to look as well as what to write.

Papers: PLANT, MISTILL

Don’t Pay Attention, PLANT It: Pretraining Attention via Learning-to-Rank

Debjyoti Saha Roy, Byron C. Wallace (opens in a new tab), Javed A. Aslam (opens in a new tab)

Tasks
ICD coding (MIMIC-IV), legal topic classification (EUR-Lex), content recommendation (Wiki10-31K)
LLM backbones
Mistral-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, DeepSeek-V3, Phi-3-mini
Abstract

State-of-the-art Extreme Multi-Label Text Classification models rely on multi-label attention to focus on key tokens in input text, but learning good attention weights is challenging. We introduce PLANT (Pretrained and Leveraged AtteNTion), a plug-and-play strategy for initializing the label-attention module that operates on top of an LLM’s output representations. PLANT works by planting label-specific attention using a Learning-to-Rank model trained on mutual information gain, before end-to-end fine-tuning. This architecture-agnostic approach integrates with LLM backbones (e.g., Mistral-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, DeepSeek-V3, and Phi-3-mini). PLANT outperforms SOTA methods across tasks like ICD coding, legal topic classification, and content recommendation, and improves fine-tuned LLMs in controlled comparisons outside the medical domain. Gains are largest on rare labels, although a training-free LLM extraction baseline remains competitive on rarely seen labels. Ablation studies confirm that attention initialization is a key driver of these gains.

Characterize Then Distill: Mechanistic Reasoning in Large Output Spaces

Debjyoti Saha Roy (sole author)

Abstract

Reasoning-trained language models can pick a document’s few labels from thousands of candidates, zero-shot. We ask which components make these choices, and whether a student can be taught where to look as well as what to write, so its decisions trace to a few components and what they read. We study clinical coding of MIMIC-IV discharge summaries with all 5,651 candidate codes in the prompt. Each choice is one token: a region of the code list, a code within it, or the departure from a similar code named earlier. We measure each attention head’s causal effect there by removing it and transplanting it into another summary. On essentially every summary, a small, shared set of heads, partly specific to each kind of choice, is necessary and sufficient; blocking what the tested heads read removes their effect. MISTILL builds on this: besides the teacher’s text, it supervises where the student attends at the teacher’s decision tokens. In a cross-family student, its own heads, found after training, restore nearly twice as much of the choice between similar codes as before, where chain-of-thought distillation alone adds under a third; one that already carried most of it gains a little, consistently across seeds, and both gains hold on the students’ own completed traces. On two corpora with coder-marked evidence, a MISTILL student’s decision heads attend to it more, and more specifically, than a chain-of-thought-distilled student’s. Controls locate the gain in supervising attention at the decision tokens; in the cross-family student, a target built from unselected teacher heads loses it. No task difference from chain-of-thought distillation is detected at matched precision. The structure recurs in a second reasoner; ranked heads beat random ones on legal-document labelling and code generation; and without retraining the students keep their gain on Spanish case reports and a QA benchmark.

About

I’m a PhD candidate in Computer Science at Northeastern University’s Khoury College of Computer Sciences, advised by Javed A. Aslam (opens in a new tab) and Byron C. Wallace (opens in a new tab).

I study how language models learn and reason when the answer has to be picked from an enormous space of possibilities (thousands of diagnosis codes, legal topics or products), where most labels have only a handful of training examples.

I work on understanding what happens inside these models (which attention heads actually make a decision) and on using that understanding to make smaller models better: PLANT (NeurIPS 2026) plants label-specific attention before fine-tuning, and MISTILL distills not only what a reasoning model writes but where it looks.

Before Northeastern, I earned an M.S. at the University of Illinois Chicago, worked as a research engineer at Xerox Research Centre India, and studied computer science at IIT Delhi (M.Tech) and IIT Patna (B.Tech).

MISTILL: the figure, its terms and the test

Step 1 A coding decision is one token

decision margin
: how strongly the model prefers the token it wrote over its competitors (Sec. 2.2)
global set
: the same top-k heads for every note, one ranking per kind of decision (Sec. 2.3)
necessity
(mean-ablation): the share of the margin lost when a set of heads is replaced by its average output
span bucket
: a stretch of text (an inventory line, a note window, a trace sentence) over which attention is summed (Sec. 4.1)
recovery
(knock-in; Fig. 2, bottom): the share of the decision restored when the set’s outputs are copied into another note
own-heads recovery
(M4): the recovery of a student’s own top heads, found after training; M1 and M4 are defined in Sec. 4.2
