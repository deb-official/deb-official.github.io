Don't Pay Attention, PLANT It

Pretraining Attention via Learning-to-Rank

Models that pick a few labels out of thousands usually give each label its own attention over the text, and start that attention from random weights. PLANT first pretrains it to rank the tokens that say the most about each label, and then fine-tunes as usual.

The problem

Thousands of labels, long documents, and a long tail

Extreme multi-label text classification (XMTC) assigns to a document the relevant few out of a very large set of labels. Medical coding is a typical case: a coder reads a discharge summary and assigns its ICD codes for diagnoses and procedures. The paper's main benchmarks, MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV, are public sets of de-identified discharge summaries with their codes.

Three things make this hard. The label space is enormous. The documents are long, and only a small fraction of their tokens matter for any one label. And most labels are rare, so the model sees them only a handful of times.

  • ≈154,000codes defined by ICD-10-CM and ICD-10-PCS together
  • 1,375median words in a MIMIC-III discharge summary
  • 61%of MIMIC-III's codes (5,411 of 8,929) appear fewer than 10 times

The standard fix: one spotlight per label

Almost every strong XMTC model adds a label-attention layer. Each label owns a query vector, and that query scores every token of the document. The result is a separate, label-specific summary of the text, so a faint cue for a rare label is not drowned out by the common ones.

The layer matters. Removing it from a product-tagging model cost 15.69 points of P@1 (Xiong et al., 2023).

The gap: every spotlight starts out random

State-of-the-art models initialize this layer randomly. Every label then has to learn, from scratch, how to rank all the tokens it might see. Rare labels have the least data to learn it from, and they are where current models fail: for PLM-ICD on MIMIC-IV, codes seen about 10 times in training have near-zero F1 (Edin et al., 2023).

If the attention layer matters this much, how it is initialized should matter too.

The method

Plant the attention first, then train the model

PLANT (Pretrained and Leveraged AtteNTion) changes how training starts, not the architecture. The label attention is an external cross-attention layer that reads the LLM's output hidden states. It is separate from the self-attention inside the LLM, which PLANT does not modify.

Architecture · what trains in each stage

Stage 1 · plantPretrain the attention as a ranker

Only the token scores S are used. For each label, the layer is trained to rank the tokens that carry the most mutual information about that label above the rest. The LoRA adapters, the label embeddings and the attention weights train. At most 10 epochs, stopped early on validation nDCG@k.

Stage 2 · fine-tuneTrain everything on the real task

The whole model trains end to end on the classification loss, starting from the Stage 1 weights instead of random ones. The loss is a focal loss with label smoothing, computed over each note's positive labels and its 1,000 hardest negatives.

What you addOne training stage before your usual fine-tuning, with a ranking loss on the label-attention scores. The attention head is the same one the single-stage baseline uses, so no parameters are added.
What it needsThe mutual information between tokens and labels, computed once on CPU from your labeled training set.
What it costs12–28% more training time on MIMIC-IV (18.7 → 22.9 hours for Mistral-7B) and, in the paper's measurements, about 10% more inference time.

Stage 1, step by step

How do you tell an attention layer what to look at?

Stage 1 needs two things: a target that says which tokens matter for each label, and a loss that teaches the attention scores to respect it. PLANT takes the target from mutual information and the loss from learning-to-rank.

Step A · the target

Mutual information gain (MIG) finds the tokens that tell on a label

For a label and a vocabulary token , count across the training set how often each one is present, and how often they occur together. The mutual information between the two events is the token's relevance for the label:

Counting co-occurrences alone would favour frequent words. Mutual information asks a sharper question: how much does seeing this token change what I believe about the label? Try the paper's worked example below.

What about “no pneumonia”? Mutual information is computed for single tokens, so a general negation word such as no gets little relevance for any one label. The paper's argument is that the attention reads the LLM's contextual hidden states, in which the vector for pneumonia can already carry the negation, and that Stage 2 can correct an imperfect Stage 1 prior. This has not been tested with a controlled negation analysis.

Step B · the loss

Train the attention scores to rank tokens in that order

For label , take two positions and in the document whose tokens are in , with the more relevant. The attention score should then be higher than . The loss sums over all such pairs, and weights each one by how much it matters to nDCG, a standard 0-to-1 score of a ranking in which mistakes near the top cost the most:

nDCG in brief. Take a ranked list. Add up each item's relevance divided by a discount that grows with its position, log2(position + 1), and divide by the best total any ordering could reach. The result is 1 for a perfect ranking, and a mistake near the top lowers it the most. “@k” means the list is cut off after k items.

Results

The same model, started differently, ends up better

Each comparison below changes one thing. The first holds the backbone and the Stage 2 loss fixed and adds Stage 1.

F1 balances precision (how many of the assigned labels are right) and recall (how many of the right labels are found). Macro-F1 averages F1 over labels, so a rare label counts as much as a common one; Micro-F1 pools every decision. P@k is the share of the k highest-scored labels that are correct. AUC measures how well true labels are scored above the others.

How can a label with no training examples score at all?In experiment 2 the rare labels get no gradient in either stage. What they share with the common labels is the attention layer's weights and the final linear layer, which are the same for every label. A held-out label still has its own query vector, at its initial value, and reads the note through those shared attention weights. When Stage 1 has trained the weights on the common labels, that is enough for 6.9–8.1% F1. When they start random, it is 0.5–1.1%.

Ablations

Which part does the work?

Each row removes or replaces one component of the full method and reports what is lost. Removing Stage 1 costs the most. Inside Stage 1, both choices matter: mutual information beats raw token frequency as the target, and the ranking loss beats regressing the scores with MSE.

Those two rows are also a control. They keep all of Stage 1's extra training and change only what it learns, and most of the gain goes away: −2.5 and −3.0 Macro-F1, against −3.5 for removing Stage 1. Extra training alone does not explain the result.

The quality of the planted attention also carries over. As Stage 1 trains longer on MIMIC-IV, its nDCG@k rises from 0.68 to 0.94, and Macro-F1 after Stage 2 rises with it.

Limitations

What PLANT does not do

It needs labelsMutual information is estimated from token and label co-occurrences in labeled training data. PLANT cannot pretrain attention without supervision, and very sparse or noisy labels make the estimates unreliable.
Scale is untested beyond 31K labelsLarger benchmarks such as Wiki-500K and Amazon-3M were not evaluated. Outside medicine, the full-dataset results use DistilBERT, and the LLM comparisons are single runs on 600-document subsets.
It does not always win on rare labelsA training-free extract-then-link pipeline beats PLANT on few-shot-label P@5 in MIMIC-IV-few, and PLANT does not improve Phi-3-small's few-shot bucket.
Negation is argued, not measuredThe paper's account of negated mentions relies on contextualized hidden states and has no controlled analysis behind it. Planted attention is also specific to a dataset; transfer across label spaces is untested.
It costs timeOn MIMIC-IV, Stage 1 adds 12–28% to training time (18.7 → 22.9 hours for Mistral-7B). The paper also measures about 10% more inference time than the vanilla fine-tuned LLMs.
